About Me

I am a postdoctoral researcher at CUNY and the American Museum of Natural History, specializing in the application of deep machine learning to time-domain astrophysics. My research focuses on quasar variability, strongly lensed quasars and galaxies, and the development of novel machine learning methods for astrophysical data analysis.

I completed my Ph.D. in Physics at the CUNY Graduate Center under the supervision of Professor Matthew O'Dowd, where I developed physics-informed neural networks and latent stochastic differential equations for modeling quasar variability and inferring black hole properties. My work has contributed to our understanding of accretion disk structure, gravitational microlensing, and dark matter substructure.

I am passionate about advancing the field of time-domain astronomy through the development of cutting-edge machine learning techniques, particularly in preparation for the Legacy Survey of Space and Time (LSST) era. My research combines theoretical astrophysics, simulation-based inference, and deep learning to extract fundamental physics from astronomical observations.

Research Interests

Machine Learning Methods

Developing novel deep learning techniques including transformers, latent SDEs/ODEs, RNNs, CNNs, variational autoencoders, normalizing flows, and simulation-based inference for astrophysical applications.

Quasar Variability

Modeling quasar variability and accretion disk reprocessing using physics-informed neural networks and Bayesian methods to infer black hole properties and understand AGN physics.

Strong Gravitational Lensing

Using machine learning to study microlensing of strongly lensed quasars, predict high-magnification events, and measure dark matter substructure in galaxy-galaxy lenses.

Time-Domain Astronomy

Preparing for the LSST era by developing techniques for analyzing time-series data, focusing on transient phenomena and variable sources across the night sky.
